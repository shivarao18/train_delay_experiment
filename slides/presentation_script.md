# Presentation Script - ASEE 2027 Proposal

## Slide 1: Teaching Trustworthy AI Decision-Making in Civil Engineering through Train-Delay Forecasting

Good afternoon, everyone. Today I am presenting a proposal for a project-based learning module that teaches undergraduate civil-engineering students something our curricula rarely cover: not how to build an AI model, but how to decide whether an AI prediction is reliable enough to act on. The vehicle for this is train-delay forecasting on a real national railway network. Everything I will show you today - every number and every figure - comes from a working technical pipeline we have already built and validated, so this proposal is grounded in a functioning prototype, not just an idea.

## Slide 2: Motivation: accuracy is not reliability

Why does this matter? AI models are already embedded in transportation systems, asset management, and structural monitoring. Our courses teach students to train these models and evaluate them with average-error metrics like MAE. The problem is that average error tells you almost nothing about the cases that matter most. A model can look excellent on average and still fail during exactly the disruptions and unusual conditions where engineers depend on it. In safety-critical infrastructure, a precise-looking prediction that carries no statement of its own uncertainty is genuinely dangerous. So the gap we are addressing is educational: students graduate knowing how to build predictive models, but not how to judge when a prediction deserves their trust.

## Slide 3: Educational objective and learning outcomes

The objective is professional judgment, not just technique. After completing the module, students should be able to distinguish accuracy from reliability, interpret prediction intervals and their coverage, read a reliability diagram that compares what a model claims against what it delivers, and - most importantly - recognize when a model that looks good on average is quietly failing in specific operating regimes. They should also be able to explain how temporal drift degrades a deployed model, and recommend appropriate engineering responses when a prediction is too uncertain or too poorly calibrated to act on. These outcomes map directly onto the four modules I will walk through next.

## Slide 4: Learning context: the RIDE railway dataset

The technical foundation is the RIDE dataset - an open, nationwide-scale record of the Belgian railway network with about 95 million train events from 2023 through 2025. The prediction task is concrete and intuitive: for every active train, predict how its delay will change at each of its next fifteen scheduled stops, roughly forty minutes into the future. What makes RIDE ideal for teaching reliability is its forward-in-time design: models are developed on 2023 and 2024 operations and tested on 2025. Temporal drift is not something we simulate - it is baked into the benchmark. Students receive starter code and partially prepared data with interpretable features in physical units - seconds of delay, trains on nearby track segments - so their effort goes into evaluating reliability, not wrangling data.

## Slide 5: Module 1 - Prediction accuracy (and its blind spots)

Module one starts where most courses end: train a model, measure MAE. Students use a scaffolded XGBoost workflow and compare it against the humblest possible baseline - assume the train's current delay simply persists. In our pilot run on over two million 2025 predictions, the model beats the baseline: 88 seconds of mean absolute error versus 98, and it wins at every forecast horizon. But then students dig into the breakdown, and the story gets interesting. In the most common situation, where the delay barely changes, the naive baseline is more than twice as accurate as the machine-learning model. The model earns its keep during large disruptions. That is the first lesson: one average number cannot tell you where a model is strong and where it is weak.

## Slide 6: Module 2 - Prediction uncertainty via conformal prediction

Module two replaces single-value predictions with prediction intervals, using conformal prediction through the MAPIE library. We deliberately give students two contrasting methods. Split conformal wraps the point model with intervals of essentially constant width and guarantees average coverage. Conformalized quantile regression adapts its width case by case. On the 2025 test year, both methods deliver almost exactly the coverage they promise - the reliability diagram on the right sits right on the diagonal - about ninety point five percent for split conformal and ninety point one for CQR at the ninety percent level. The difference is sharpness: CQR achieves the same coverage with meaningfully narrower typical intervals. Students learn that two honest models can price their uncertainty very differently, and sharper honesty is worth more.

## Slide 7: Module 3 - Temporal drift and conditional reliability

Module three asks the question that separates reliability from accuracy: when a method says ninety percent coverage, does that hold within specific operating conditions, or only on average? The answer, shown in these plots, is striking. Split conformal keeps its ninety percent promise on average - but that average is a blend of ninety-nine percent coverage for five-minute-ahead predictions and only seventy-one percent for predictions more than forty-five minutes out. For heavily delayed trains, coverage also drops to seventy-one percent - exactly the trains a dispatcher cares most about. CQR, because it widens its intervals where the task is harder, holds between eighty-six and ninety-three percent across all conditions. Students see with their own data that a marginal guarantee can hide conditional failure, and that drift across the 2025 test year is real but modest compared to these regime effects.

## Slide 8: Module 4 - Engineering decision-making with real cases

Module four turns the analysis into engineering judgment. Instead of inventing hypothetical vignettes, we mine them from the model's own 2025 predictions - these are real trains on real days. Scenario A: the point prediction was off by just twelve seconds, but the interval spanned nearly ten minutes - accurate, yet the model knew it was uncertain. There are twenty-four thousand cases like it. Scenario B is the dangerous opposite: a confident, narrow interval that simply missed. Scenario C shows the aggregate illusion - during congested disruptions, error nearly triples and coverage sags. Scenario D follows one train: the half-minute-ahead interval was a hundred seconds wide; the twenty-seven-minute one, over twelve hundred. For each case students must choose: act on the prediction, hedge, or escalate to a human. That is the professional skill we are targeting.

## Slide 9: Research questions and assessment plan

The study itself asks five research questions: whether the module improves students' understanding of the accuracy-reliability distinction; whether they can correctly interpret intervals, coverage, calibration, and reliability diagrams; whether they can carry those concepts into realistic decision scenarios; which concepts prove hardest to learn - our candidates are temporal drift, conditional coverage, calibration, and interval width; and how the experience shapes their attitudes toward AI in practice. Assessment is mixed-methods: pre- and post-tests - for example, choosing between a model with lower error but poor coverage and one with slightly higher error but honest intervals - plus rubric-scored assignments, scenario-based decision tasks, attitude surveys, and an optional transfer task to another domain such as structural-health monitoring. IRB approval will be in place before any student data is collected.

## Slide 10: Expected contribution and current status

To close: the contribution is a reproducible module that teaches civil engineers to evaluate AI the way the profession will need - recognizing unreliable predictions and making defensible decisions under uncertainty - together with evidence about which of these concepts students find hardest, which should be useful to anyone integrating trustworthy-AI topics into an engineering curriculum. I want to emphasize that the technical foundation is not speculative: the full pipeline - open data, open tooling, scripted end to end - already runs, and every figure and number you saw today came out of it. What remains is the educational study itself: finalizing the student-facing materials, piloting the module, and collecting the assessment data. Thank you - I would be glad to take questions.
