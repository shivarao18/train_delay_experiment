# Research Proposal

## Teaching Trustworthy AI Decision-Making in Civil Engineering through Train-Delay Forecasting

### Background and Motivation
Artificial intelligence and machine-learning models are increasingly used in transportation systems, infrastructure management, structural monitoring, and other areas of civil engineering. Engineering education must therefore move beyond teaching students only how to train predictive models. Students must also learn how to determine whether an AI prediction is sufficiently reliable for use in real engineering decisions.

Traditional model evaluation often emphasizes average-error measures such as Mean Absolute Error. However, a model can have low average error while performing poorly during disruptions, congestion, or unfamiliar operating conditions. In safety- and reliability-sensitive infrastructure, a precise-looking prediction may be misleading when the model does not communicate its uncertainty.

This project proposes a project-based learning module that uses real-world train-delay data to teach undergraduate civil-engineering students the difference between prediction accuracy and prediction reliability. Train-delay forecasting offers an accessible educational context because prediction uncertainty changes over time, generally increases with forecast horizon, and can be affected by congestion and cascading delays. The proposed module builds on the initial curriculum structure and assessment plan developed for this study.

### Educational Objective
The objective is to help students develop the technical understanding and professional judgment required to evaluate AI systems used in civil infrastructure.

After completing the module, students should be able to:
* Distinguish prediction accuracy from prediction reliability.
* Interpret prediction intervals, coverage, calibration, sharpness, and reliability diagrams.
* Identify when a model performs well overall but fails under specific operating conditions.
* Explain how temporal drift can reduce the reliability of an AI model.
* Recommend appropriate engineering responses when model predictions are uncertain or poorly calibrated.

### Participants and Learning Context
The module is intended for undergraduate civil-engineering students enrolled in [course name] at the University of Florida. Approximately [number of students] students are expected to participate. The module is provisionally designed to be delivered over three to four weeks, although its duration may be adjusted to fit the course schedule.

Students will use a scaffolded workflow based on the public RIDE railway dataset (Elliker et al., 2026), an open, nationwide-scale dataset and benchmark of passenger railway operations over the Belgian railway network covering 2023 through 2025. RIDE provides model-ready benchmark data with a fixed forward-in-time evaluation design: models are developed on 2023-2024 operations and evaluated on 2025 operations, so temporal drift is built into the task rather than simulated. Starter code and partially prepared data will be provided so that the primary emphasis remains on evaluating reliability and making engineering decisions rather than on extensive data cleaning or advanced model development.

### Module Design
The learning intervention will consist of four sequential modules.

**Module 1: Prediction Accuracy in Civil Infrastructure**
Students will use a scaffolded XGBoost workflow to train or evaluate a train-delay prediction model. They will initially assess performance using Mean Absolute Error, compare the model against a naive baseline that assumes a train's current delay persists, and examine why low average error does not guarantee that every prediction is dependable. In particular, students will observe that the learned model outperforms the baseline overall and during disruptions, yet the naive baseline remains more accurate in the most common operating regime, where delays barely change.

**Module 2: Understanding Prediction Uncertainty**
Students will compare single-value predictions with prediction intervals generated by conformal prediction methods. Two deliberately contrasting approaches will be used: split conformal prediction, which produces intervals of nearly constant width with a marginal coverage guarantee, and conformalized quantile regression, which produces adaptive intervals that widen when predictions are genuinely harder. Students will learn to interpret interval coverage, interval width, calibration, sharpness, underconfidence, and overconfidence. Reliability diagrams - here, calibration curves comparing the stated (nominal) coverage of prediction intervals against their observed (empirical) coverage - will be used to show whether a model's stated uncertainty agrees with its observed performance.

**Module 3: Temporal Drift and Conditional Reliability**
Students will evaluate models chronologically, following the RIDE benchmark design: model development and interval calibration use 2023-2024 railway data, and testing uses 2025 data. They will examine how reliability changes across forecast horizons and operational conditions, including normal versus delayed trains, low- versus high-congestion periods, and successive months of the test year as the data drifts further from the training period.

The focus will be on conditional reliability rather than algorithmic fairness. Students will investigate whether strong average performance hides poor performance within specific operating regimes - for example, whether a 90% prediction interval that covers 90% of cases on average still covers 90% of cases for heavily delayed trains during congested periods.

**Module 4: Engineering Decision-Making**
Students will apply their technical findings to realistic infrastructure scenarios mined from actual model predictions on the 2025 test data. They will determine appropriate responses when:
* A prediction is accurate but highly uncertain.
* A prediction interval is narrow but poorly calibrated.
* A model performs well overall but is unreliable during disruptions.
* Long-horizon predictions are less dependable than short-horizon predictions.

This module connects AI evaluation with professional engineering judgment and responsible infrastructure decision-making.

### Research Questions
The study will investigate the following questions:
1. Does the project-based module improve students' understanding of the difference between prediction accuracy and prediction reliability?
2. Can students correctly interpret prediction intervals, coverage, calibration, sharpness, and reliability diagrams after completing the module?
3. Can students apply AI-reliability concepts to realistic civil-infrastructure decision-making scenarios?
4. Which concepts - temporal drift, conditional coverage, calibration, or interval width - are most difficult for students to understand?
5. How do students perceive the relevance of uncertainty-aware AI to their education and future civil-engineering practice?

### Evaluation and Assessment
A mixed-methods assessment framework will be used to evaluate student learning.

* Pre- and post-tests will measure changes in students' understanding of prediction accuracy, uncertainty, and calibration. For example, students may be asked to compare a model with lower average error but poor interval coverage against a model with slightly higher error but reliable uncertainty estimates, or to choose between two interval methods with similar coverage but very different sharpness (constant-width versus adaptive intervals).
* Rubric-scored assignments will assess whether students can correctly interpret reliability diagrams, compare prediction intervals, identify conditional model failures, and justify engineering recommendations.
* Scenario-based decision tasks will require students to select or reject AI predictions in realistic railway and infrastructure situations.
* Attitudinal surveys will measure student confidence, perceived relevance, and attitudes toward using AI in civil-engineering practice.
* An optional knowledge-transfer task will ask students to apply the same concepts to another domain, such as structural-health monitoring or roadway traffic prediction.

Appropriate institutional and ethical approval will be obtained before student information is collected or analyzed for research purposes.

### Expected Contribution
The expected contribution is a reproducible educational module for teaching trustworthy and responsible AI evaluation to undergraduate civil engineers. Unlike instructional activities that focus only on model accuracy or coding performance, this study emphasizes whether students can recognize unreliable predictions and make appropriate professional decisions under uncertainty.

The railway application provides a realistic but manageable context for introducing temporal drift, conditional performance, and uncertainty-aware engineering judgment. The study may also identify which AI-reliability concepts require additional instructional support and provide guidance for integrating trustworthy AI topics into civil-engineering curricula.
